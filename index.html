Open Science Issue Areas

COS's advocacy efforts center on three core policy areas.

Below each issue area, we've shared some core talking points that we use throughout our advocacy materials. You can adapt these talking points — and draw from our more details documents — in your own advocacy efforts.

01 Issue area one

Adopt and implement the TOP Guidelines

The Transparency and Openness Promotion (TOP) Guidelines is a policy framework to increase the verifiability of empirical of research claims developed by the TOP Advisory Board in collaboration with COS. Policymakers at journals, publishers, funders, and institutions can look to TOP for recommended language and actions for advancing research.

Progress has been made in advancing policies that increase access to the published literature and underlying data. Policymakers have a significant opportunity to increase access to the rest of the research lifecycle, including study plans, protocols, code, and materials. The TOP Guidelines and associated implementation guidance provide a tiered framework for promoting research practices so the path from question to claim can be followed and independently verified.

Open science policies are only as effective as their specificty and the degree to which they consider monitoring compliance. The TOP Guidelines have a modular design, with research practices that can be implemented at three levels of increasing stringency—disclose, share and cite, and certify—so a policy can state not only that a practice is expected, but at what level and how compliance will be verified. This design ensures a policy is not just a statement of values, but can be meaningully implemented.

Define “scientific data” functionally: every digital item needed to validate and replicate the finding, including analytic code, codebooks, and survey instruments.

Findings that were never published are still knowledge. Agency plans should cover data not tied to a peer-reviewed publication — knowing what was tested and shelved is what corrects a biased literature.

02 Issue area two

Advance researcher assessment reform

Hiring, promotion, and funding criteria are the strongest signal a research system sends. Right now they reward volume and novelty over reliability.

Reward the practices, not just the outputs: preregistration, shared data and code, replication, and reported null results should count in review.

Ask evaluators to judge a small number of specific contributions rather than proxy metrics like journal prestige or citation counts.

Source: Response to NIH RFI: Measuring and Rewarding Scientific Impact

Publication bias is an incentive problem before it is a publishing problem. If null findings carry no career value, they stay in the drawer.

Source: Response to RFI on Reducing Publication Bias Against Null Studies

Assessment reform is cheap relative to its effect — it changes behavior across an entire portfolio without new infrastructure spending.

03 Issue area three

Fund open research infrastructure sustainably

Mandates do not produce access on their own. Repositories, identifiers, and the links between records need funding that outlasts a grant cycle.

Discoverability is the real test of access. Content sitting in a repository nobody searches is not accessible in any useful sense.

Source: Response to NSF RFI: Public Access Plan 2.0

Build bi-directional links between publisher platforms, preprint servers, and agency repositories, so funded work is the first thing a reader finds — or one click away from it.

Source: Response to NSF RFI: Public Access Plan 2.0

Publishing-cost policy and access policy interact. Limiting allowable fees without funding free routes to publish just moves the barrier.

Source: Response to NOT-OD-25-138: Proposal to Limit Allowable Publishing Costs

Community-governed, non-profit infrastructure is more resilient than infrastructure tied to a single vendor, platform, or award.

Research Integrity and Peer Review Aug 2026

TOP 2025: An update to the Transparency and Openness Promotion Guidelines

Transparency and openness are core scientific values that enable independent verification of reported research results. The Transparency and Openness Promotion (TOP) Guidelines, first published in 2015 (TOP 2015), provided a flexible framework for journals to design and implement publication standards promoting these values. Over the past decade, widespread use of TOP 2015 contributed to valuable evidence and feedback for refinement.
